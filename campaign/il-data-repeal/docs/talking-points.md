# Talking Points — Three-Prong Strategy

## How to Use This Document

Organized by the three campaign prongs: **Lobby**, **Grassroots**, and **Federal/Legal**. For each prong: key messages, anticipated objections, and prepared responses. Memorize the responses to the top 3 objections — they will come up in every conversation.

---

## Prong 1: Lobby (Legislators and Staff)

### Key Messages

1. **HB 5798 is a clean, two-sentence repeal.** No amendments, no complexity. "The Digital Asset Tax Act is repealed. Effective immediately." This is the simplest possible legislative action.

2. **The tax raises 0.11% of the state budget while risking an entire industry.** $60 million in projected revenue against a $53 billion budget. The crypto industry's direct and indirect economic contribution to Illinois — jobs, events, tax revenue from existing mechanisms — almost certainly exceeds $60 million.

3. **No other state does this.** Illinois is the first and only state to impose a transaction-level tax on digital assets. Every other state that has considered it has rejected it. This is not a trend; it's an outlier.

4. **The tax is on volume, not profits — that's the fundamental problem.** Capital gains taxes are standard and fair. Transaction taxes on total value are punitive and economically destructive. Even Wall Street killed the financial transaction tax proposals because they understood this.

5. **The fall veto session is the window.** Once January 1 arrives and the tax takes effect, repeal becomes exponentially harder — it becomes a "revenue cut" rather than "preventing a bad tax." Act now while it's still preventive.

### Anticipated Objections & Responses

**Objection: "It's only 0.2%. That's tiny."**

> Response: 0.2% per transaction compounds rapidly. A single DeFi swap can involve 3-5 underlying transactions. An active user making 10 operations per month on $5,000 positions pays $500-1,200/year — even if their portfolio declines. Think of it this way: if your bank charged you 0.2% every time you transferred money between your own accounts, you'd switch banks. Illinois residents can't switch states as easily, but the businesses, events, and developers can — and will.

**Objection: "We need the revenue. Illinois has a budget problem."**

> Response: $60 million is real money, and we don't dismiss the state's fiscal challenges. But consider the trade-off: Illinois already collects income tax on crypto profits, sales tax on crypto-related business activity, and property tax from crypto companies with offices here. The Digital Asset Tax will drive the industry that generates all of that existing revenue out of state. The net fiscal impact is almost certainly negative. You don't solve a revenue problem by chasing away the tax base.

**Objection: "This only affects big traders and exchanges."**

> Response: The tax applies to anyone who uses an exchange or interacts with decentralized finance — including individuals managing their own savings. A person who swaps $500 of one cryptocurrency for another on a Saturday morning owes $1 in tax on that single transaction. That same person rebalancing monthly owes $12/year just on small swaps. Scale that up to normal portfolio management and it becomes significant. This isn't a Wall Street tax; it's a tax on the person in Naperville managing their retirement savings.

**Objection: "Crypto is used for crime / speculation / gambling."**

> Response: The vast majority of crypto transactions are legitimate financial activity — savings, investment, remittances, and commercial payments. The federal government regulates digital assets through the SEC and CFTC precisely because they are legitimate financial instruments. If the concern is illicit activity, law enforcement tools already exist. Taxing all transactions doesn't stop crime; it punishes the law-abiding majority.

**Objection: "We can amend it instead of repealing."**

> Response: We welcome the conversation about good crypto policy. But the current framework is so fundamentally flawed — taxing volume rather than gains, applying to an asset class that no other state taxes this way — that amendment would be building on a broken foundation. Repeal first, then have the policy conversation about what, if anything, should replace it.

## Prong 2: Grassroots (Constituents and Community)

### Key Messages

1. **Your state rep needs to hear from you during recess.** July through October, legislators are in their districts. Town halls, phone calls, and letters from constituents are what move votes. If your rep doesn't hear about this issue, they'll assume nobody cares.

2. **You don't need to be a crypto expert to care about this.** This is about fair taxation. No one's savings account gets taxed every time they move money. Crypto shouldn't either.

3. **OnionDAO and events like it bring real money to your neighborhood.** 400+ people spending a month in Chicago means restaurants, venues, Airbnbs, rideshares, and local businesses getting paid. The Digital Asset Tax tells these events to go somewhere else.

4. **The numbers make the case.** $60 million (0.11% of the budget) in exchange for driving away an industry worth multiples of that. Show up, say the numbers, and ask: "Is that a good trade?"

5. **November is the deadline.** The veto session (est. Nov 12-21) is when HB 5798 can pass. After January 1, the tax is law and much harder to undo.

### Anticipated Objections & Responses

**Objection: "I don't own crypto, so why should I care?"**

> Response: You may not own crypto, but you benefit from the businesses and events that the crypto community brings to your town. OnionDAO puts money into Chicago restaurants, venues, and local businesses for an entire month. That benefits workers and owners whether or not they personally hold any cryptocurrency. Driving this community out of state means driving out their spending too.

**Objection: "Rich people should pay more taxes."**

> Response: This tax doesn't target wealthy people. It targets anyone who uses digital assets — including people using crypto for remittances (sending money to family abroad), small savers, and freelancers who get paid in crypto. The 0.2% rate hits small transactions proportionally just as hard as large ones. A college student swapping $100 pays the same rate as a hedge fund moving millions. And neither of them pays this kind of tax on stocks.

**Objection: "Just use a different state / don't use crypto."**

> Response: "Use a different state" is exactly the problem. When Illinois makes it more expensive to do business here than in any other state, people and businesses take that advice. That's not a solution — that's an economic development failure. And "don't use crypto" is like saying "don't use the internet" in 1998. Digital assets are an increasingly mainstream part of the financial system.

**Objection: "I went to my rep's town hall and they didn't know about this."**

> Response: That's actually an opportunity. If they don't know about it, you get to be the person who informs them. Bring the one-pager, explain the bank account analogy, and ask them to look into HB 5798. Most legislators genuinely want to hear from constituents on issues they haven't formed an opinion on yet.

## Prong 3: Federal/Legal (Constitutional and Federal Preemption)

### Key Messages

1. **The Commerce Clause prohibits states from unduly burdening interstate commerce.** Digital asset transactions occur on global, decentralized networks. Illinois is taxing transactions that touch the state only because the user is physically here — the transaction infrastructure, counterparties, and liquidity are elsewhere.

2. **The Internet Tax Freedom Act prohibits discriminatory taxes on electronic commerce.** The Digital Asset Tax singles out one form of electronic financial transaction (crypto) while leaving equivalent electronic transactions (stocks, wire transfers, foreign exchange) untaxed. This is precisely the discrimination the ITFA was designed to prevent.

3. **Equal Protection requires similarly situated activities to be treated the same.** Buying $10,000 of Bitcoin and selling at a profit triggers both income tax AND a 0.2% transaction tax. Buying $10,000 of Apple stock and selling at a profit triggers only income tax. There is no rational basis for this distinction.

4. **Wayfair (the Supreme Court sales tax case) does not save Illinois.** Wayfair addressed whether online retailers must collect sales tax. DeFi has no retailer, no identifiable seller, and no commercial relationship to tax. The frameworks are incompatible.

5. **A legal challenge is viable and likely.** Multiple organizations (Coin Center, DeFi Education Fund, Blockchain Association) have the resources and track record to bring Constitutional challenges. If the law takes effect, it will be litigated — costing Illinois taxpayers the expense of defending it.

### Anticipated Objections & Responses

**Objection: "States have broad taxing authority."**

> Response: They do, within Constitutional limits. States cannot tax in ways that discriminate against interstate commerce (Commerce Clause), discriminate against electronic commerce (ITFA), or treat similarly situated activities differently without rational basis (Equal Protection). The Digital Asset Tax implicates all three. Broad taxing authority is not unlimited taxing authority.

**Objection: "The legislature's legal team reviewed this."**

> Response: Legislative review is not the same as judicial review. Many laws that pass legislative scrutiny are later struck down by courts. The Digital Asset Tax is unprecedented — no state has tried this before — which means there is no case law directly upholding it, and significant case law that calls its foundations into question.

**Objection: "Federal crypto regulation is unsettled, so states can act."**

> Response: Federal regulation is actively developing, not absent. The CFTC and SEC both assert jurisdiction over digital assets. The CFTC Chair has publicly opposed state transaction taxes on crypto. As federal frameworks solidify, state-level transaction taxes are increasingly likely to face preemption challenges. Illinois is building on sand.

## Prong 4: Tax Revenue Engine (Cross-Prong — Use Everywhere)

### Key Messages

1. **OnionDAO already generates more tax revenue for Chicago than the crypto tax would collect from community DAOs.** 30 days of catered meals (10.25% sales tax), restaurant buyouts (10.25%), hotel stays (19% — highest in the US), yacht charters (9% amusement tax), venue rentals (9% commercial lease tax). All paid. All taxed. All at risk.

2. **Chicago is spending $40 million a year to attract exactly this kind of event — while Illinois taxes it away.** The city just created a Tourism Improvement District and raised hotel taxes to 19% specifically to fund Choose Chicago's convention attraction program. OnionDAO came for free. Now the state is driving it out.

3. **OnionDAO could be Chicago's SXSW.** A month-long tech event that brings builders, learners, and spending from around the world. Austin doesn't tax the tech industry that makes SXSW possible. Miami doesn't tax the crypto industry that brings Consensus. Why is Illinois?

4. **The city has a Convention Attraction Fund that literally pays events to come to Chicago.** OnionDAO came voluntarily, brought 400 people for a full month, and spent hundreds of thousands on local businesses. The state is now taxing away the reason they came.

### Anticipated Objections & Responses

**Objection: "OnionDAO is just one event."**

> Response: It's one event that runs for 30 days, attracts 400+ people, and generates sustained daily spending across restaurants, hotels, venues, and local businesses. Per capita, the economic impact per attendee over a month likely exceeds any 3-day tech conference. And it's a signal — if OnionDAO leaves, every crypto event organizer considering Chicago sees the message: Illinois taxes the industry that funds you.

**Objection: "The crypto tax applies to exchanges, not events."**

> Response: It applies to anyone who exchanges, transfers, or stores digital assets on behalf of an Illinois customer. The organizer of OnionDAO operates in crypto. The DAO treasury that funds the event operates in crypto. The entire reason this community exists and spends money in Chicago is crypto. Tax the industry and you lose the spending. The mechanism doesn't matter — the economic effect does.

## Quick Reference: The Four Killer Responses

For any conversation, these four responses cover 90% of objections:

### 1. The Bank Account Analogy (for "It's only 0.2%")
> "Imagine if your bank charged you 0.2% every time you moved money — checking to savings, savings back to checking. Not on interest earned, on the total amount transferred. You'd lose hundreds of dollars a year just for managing your own money. That's exactly what this tax does to crypto."

### 2. The Budget Math (for "We need the revenue")
> "$60 million on a $53 billion budget. That's 0.11% — one tenth of one percent. And it comes at the cost of driving out an industry whose existing economic activity — events, jobs, business spending — generates far more than $60 million in tax revenue through mechanisms that already exist."

### 3. The Discrimination Point (for "Just tax crypto like everything else")
> "We agree. Tax crypto gains just like stock gains — Illinois already does that through income tax. What the Digital Asset Tax does is add an extra transaction tax that doesn't exist for stocks, bonds, or any other financial instrument. We're not asking for special treatment. We're asking for equal treatment."

### 4. The Golden Goose (for legislators who care about economic development)
> "Chicago just spent $40 million in new hotel tax revenue to attract conventions. OnionDAO came for free — 400 people, 30 days, restaurants bought out, hotels booked, concerts produced. The city is paying to attract what the state is taxing away. That's not revenue policy. That's friendly fire."
