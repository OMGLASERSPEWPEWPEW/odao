## Draft

Every summer, more than 400 technologists, artists, and builders descend on Chicago for OnionDAO — a month-long gathering where people from across the country live, work, and create together in the city. They buy out restaurants for community dinners. They rent venues for workshops and concerts. They charter boats, fill Airbnbs, and pour money into the local economy from Pilsen to the Loop to Hyde Park.

Starting January 1, events like this may stop coming to Illinois.

On June 16, Governor Pritzker signed Senate Bill 3019 into law, creating the Digital Asset Tax Act — a 0.2% tax on the total value of every cryptocurrency transaction conducted by Illinois residents or through Illinois-based platforms. The legislature's revenue estimate: about $60 million per year. The actual cost: potentially driving billions in economic activity out of the state.

**This is not a capital gains tax.** Illinois already taxes crypto profits through income tax, just like every other investment. The Digital Asset Tax is something entirely different — a levy on the total amount of every transaction, regardless of whether anyone made money. Imagine if Illinois charged you 0.2% every time you transferred money between your own bank accounts. That is what this law does to digital asset users.

For someone who actively manages a crypto portfolio — rebalancing, providing liquidity, participating in decentralized finance — the costs compound rapidly. A person making modest, regular transactions can easily lose $1,000 or more per year in taxes on money they never profited from. It is death by a thousand cuts, designed to extract revenue from a technology the legislature apparently does not understand.

CFTC Chair [Christy Goldsmith Romero] Selig did not mince words when she called state-level crypto transaction taxes what they are: **"sin taxes."** The federal government's top commodities regulator is telling states that taxing digital asset transactions — as opposed to gains — treats participation in a legitimate, regulated market as something to be punished. Illinois listened and signed the bill anyway.

The practical consequences are already materializing. Crypto companies evaluating office locations are crossing Illinois off the list. Event organizers are fielding calls from Austin, Miami, and Nashville — cities that would love to host the conferences and communities that currently choose Chicago. The developers, entrepreneurs, and investors who make up this ecosystem are mobile by nature. They will go where they are welcome, and they will take their spending with them.

Consider what Chicago loses if just one event like OnionDAO relocates. Four hundred people spending a month in the city. Restaurant revenue. Venue bookings. Airbnb stays. Rideshare trips. Local vendor contracts. The sales tax, hotel tax, and income tax that this spending already generates for Illinois — revenue the state collects without a special discriminatory levy.

Now multiply that by every crypto meetup, every blockchain conference, every fintech company weighing whether to stay in Illinois or join the exodus to friendlier jurisdictions.

The legislature justified this tax as a modest revenue measure. Sixty million dollars sounds significant until you realize it is **one-tenth of one percent** of the state's $53 billion budget. It is a rounding error that carries an outsized cost: the message that Illinois views an entire technology sector as a revenue extraction target rather than an economic engine.

No other asset class is treated this way. Stock trades, bond transactions, commodity futures — none carry a state transaction tax. Chicago is home to the CME Group and the CBOE, two of the largest derivatives exchanges in the world. The city understands that taxing the act of trading, rather than the profits from trading, is a recipe for driving markets elsewhere. The Digital Asset Tax ignores this lesson.

Rep. John Cabello has filed **House Bill 5798** to repeal the Digital Asset Tax Act before it takes effect. The bill is clean and direct: full repeal, effective immediately. The fall veto session, expected November 12-21, is the window.

Illinois has real budget challenges. No one denies that. But the answer is not a tax that generates marginal revenue while sending a globally visible signal that this state is hostile to innovation. The crypto industry is not asking for special treatment. It is asking for the same treatment that every other asset class already receives.

The restaurants, the venues, the neighborhoods that benefit from events like OnionDAO — they do not care whether their customers use dollars or digital assets. They care that people keep showing up.

If Illinois does not repeal this tax, they will stop showing up.
